Crocus sativus

Made from the saffron crocus — the flower whose three red threads have flavoured, coloured and medicined the world, and the classical remedy of alternating extremes.

The whole picture, in one breath

Think of a pendulum in the soul: gay, singing, dancing one hour; weeping, raging, despairing the next; a sensation as of something alive moving within; dark passive bleeding that clots in long strings; and a strange, laughing indifference to serious things. The pendulum remedy: that is Crocus.

The substance, in plain words

The saffron crocus is the most expensive spice on earth — three crimson threads per flower, harvestable only by hand. Its crocin and safranal chemistry is genuinely psychoactive in large doses: saffron is a serious modern research subject for mood, with genuine antidepressant trials behind it.

The traditional picture Classical sources

The inner state

  • Alternating moods. The keynote: sudden swings — laughter to tears, mildness to rage, within the hour; the books call it the pendulum.
  • Sensation of something alive inside. A fixed sensation as of a living thing moving in the abdomen — the celebrated Crocus sign.
  • Laughing at serious things. Inappropriate laughter and jesting; religious excitement alternating with despair.
  • Anger and striking. Sudden, inexplicable rages, striking at loved ones, then instant remorse.

The body

  • Dark bleeding in strings. The keynote: passive haemorrhage — dark, clotted, stringy blood from uterus or nose, worse the least motion.
  • Sensation of movement within. The books pair the mental 'alive-inside' sensation with real abdominal sensations.
  • Better for open air Cool air and motion steady the swings.
  • Worse warm rooms. Heat and close rooms worsen the moods and the bleeding.
  • Yawning and stretching. Constant yawning with the mood states — a quoted confirmatory sign.
  • Worse morning The swings run their own clock, independent of events.

Where it acts (organ affinities)

Mind and mood (alternation), blood (dark stringy bleeding), female organs, abdomen.

Where the classical picture comes from

The spice's psychoactive chemistry — known to the ancients, who regulated saffron's dose carefully — gave the proving its pendulum portrait; the remedy became the classical answer to alternating moods and dark stringy haemorrhages.

The substance in real medicine Pharmacology

What this section is: the pharmacology and toxicology of the source substance. It is not clinical evidence that a homeopathic preparation of it treats any condition. What controlled trials do and do not show is set out on the Evidence page.

Saffron's modern research is genuine and growing: randomised trials show antidepressant effects comparable to low-dose fluoxetine in mild-to-moderate depression — one of the strongest evidence bases in the whole spice rack. At culinary doses it flavours; at pharmacological doses it treats mood; at excessive doses it poisons. The three threads carry all three stories.
