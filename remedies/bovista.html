Bovista

Made from the puffball — the meadow fungus whose spores were a genuine battlefield styptic, and the classical remedy of the nocturnal menses.

The whole picture, in one breath

Think of a woman whose troubles run by night: menstrual flow that comes only in the dark or floods when lying down, blotchy eruptions that change with the weather, a tendency to bruise absurdly, and diarrhea before the menses. The night-flowing, blotchy remedy: that is Bovista.

The substance, in plain words

The puffball is the white meadow fungus that bursts in a cloud of spores. Those spores were a genuine battlefield and surgical styptic for centuries — packed into wounds to staunch blood — and the proving gave the remedy its strange nocturnal, blotchy portrait.

The traditional picture Classical sources

The inner state

  • Anxiety and irritability. Cross, worried, worse before the menses; the books paint the anxious, blotchy temperament.
  • Excitability. Nervous, excitable, worse in the evening.
  • Despondency. Gloomy in the chronic, recurrent states.

The body

  • Menses flow at night. The keynote: the flow comes on lying down, at night — or floods on the least recumbency; the books made it Bovista's signature.
  • Bruising absurdly. Bruises from the slightest knock — the puffball-spore styptic's paradoxical portrait.
  • Blotchy weather-changed eruptions. Eruptions that blot, spot and shift with the weather — worse washing
  • Diarrhea before menses. The bowel loosens before the flow — the quoted association.
  • Worse damp cold and new moon Wet weather and the month's turning aggravate.
  • Better for rest Dry weather and moving about ease the blotches.

Where it acts (organ affinities)

Where the classical picture comes from

The battlefield styptic's folk career gave the proving its frame; the nocturnal flow and the absurd bruising became the remedy's identity — a portrait from the meadow fungus that once packed the wounds of armies.

The substance in real medicine Pharmacology

What this section is: the pharmacology and toxicology of the source substance. It is not clinical evidence that a homeopathic preparation of it treats any condition. What controlled trials do and do not show is set out on the Evidence page.

Puffball spores were a genuine, effective styptic — used from ancient armies to nineteenth-century surgery, their fine mesh mechanically staunching blood. The menstruation it portrayed is now gynaecology's work-up — abnormal bleeding imaged, hormonally managed, surgically resolved when needed.
