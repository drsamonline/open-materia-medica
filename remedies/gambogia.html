Gambogia

Made from gamboge — the yellow gum resin of the Asian Garcinia, an official violent purgative of the pharmacopoeias, and the classical remedy of the gushing, hot-sweated purge.

The whole picture, in one breath

Think of a purge that arrives suddenly: a gushing, yellow, watery stool that comes on with griping and a hot sweat, as if the whole abdomen had turned on a tap; burning in the anus; and the books' portrait of the violent hydrogogue purge. The gushing purge with hot sweat: that is Gambogia.

The substance, in plain words

Gamboge is the bright-yellow gum resin of the Asian Garcinia tree — an official, violent hydrogogue purgative of the old pharmacopoeias, employed when the body needed emptying by force. Its resin acids genuinely grip and flush; the proving gave the remedy its gushing portrait.

The traditional picture Classical sources

The inner state

  • Anxiety with the griping. Fear during the violent evacuations, worse at night.
  • Irritability. Cross from the griping and the weakness after.
  • Despondency. Gloomy in the chronic, alternately-bound-and-loose states.

The body

  • Gushing watery stool. The keynote: sudden, profuse, yellow, watery evacuation — the tap turned full on.
  • Griping with hot sweat. The purge comes with cramping gripes and a drenching hot sweat — the books' signature.
  • Burning of the anus. Smarting and burning as the watery stool passes.
  • Worse eating and drinking. Anything by mouth restarts the tap.
  • Alternate constipation. The books note the alternation — bound for days, then the gush.
  • Better for rest Rest and warmth steady the exhausted bowel between evacuations.

Where it acts (organ affinities)

Where the classical picture comes from

The official hydrogogue's violence gave the proving its portrait — the tap-like gush, the griping, the hot sweat. Gambogia became the remedy of the sudden, profuse, exhausting purge, standing beside its own resin's real career in the dispensary.

The substance in real medicine Pharmacology

What this section is: the pharmacology and toxicology of the source substance. It is not clinical evidence that a homeopathic preparation of it treats any condition. What controlled trials do and do not show is set out on the Evidence page.

Gamboge was a genuine, official purgative — violent enough to be dangerous, effective enough to be kept — retired when saline and, later, osmotic laxatives made purging safe. The diarrhea it portrayed is now rehydration therapy and cause-finding: modern medicine's first answer to the tap turned on.
