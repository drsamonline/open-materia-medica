Petroselinum

Made from parsley — the garden herb whose apiol was a genuine, dangerous abortifacient of the old pharmacopeias, and the classical remedy of the sudden irresistible urge.

The whole picture, in one breath

Think of an urge that cannot wait: the sudden, overwhelming necessity to urinate — arising at a knock on the door, at the sight of running water, at the mere thought — with burning and tingling in the urethra, and the old books' association with the poorly managed discharges of the era. The irresistible sudden urge: that is Petroselinum.

The substance, in plain words

Parsley is the universal kitchen herb whose seed and root oil carries apiol — a genuine, dangerous drug of the old pharmacopoeias, once used as an abortifacient and emmenagogue before its toxicity ended its career. The proving gave the remedy its sudden-urge portrait.

The traditional picture Classical sources

The inner state

  • Anxiety with the urgency. Fear of being caught, of the next spasm, worse at night.
  • Irritability. Cross, impatient with the constant interruptions.
  • Despondency. Gloomy in the chronic, recurrent states.

The body

  • Sudden irresistible urging. The keynote: the necessity arrives without warning — at a sound, a sight, a thought — and cannot be denied.
  • Triggered by running water. The celebrated trigger: the sight or sound of water brings the urge — quoted in every repertory.
  • Burning, tingling urethra. A sensation of tingling and burning along the passage, worse after urination.
  • Old gonorrhoeal legacy. The books pair it with the chronic urethral states left by badly managed infection.
  • Worse cold damp and night. Wet weather and night aggravate the urging.
  • Better for rest Rest, warmth and the bladder's emptying bring brief peace.

Where it acts (organ affinities)

Bladder and urethra, urinary reflexes, mucous membranes.

Where the classical picture comes from

The kitchen herb's dangerous apiol chemistry — the abortifacient of the old poison records — gave the proving its frame; the sudden-urge signature, with its famous water-trigger, made Petroselinum unique in the urinary chapter.

The substance in real medicine Pharmacology

What this section is: the pharmacology and toxicology of the source substance. It is not clinical evidence that a homeopathic preparation of it treats any condition. What controlled trials do and do not show is set out on the Evidence page.

Apiol was a genuine, official, dangerous drug: the parsley-seed oil of the emmenagogue era, effective enough to be feared, toxic enough to be abandoned — its overdose poisonings are documented in the old literature. Modern urinary medicine treats the irritation the remedy portrayed with diagnosis and simple therapy; the apiol belongs, honourably, to history's locked cabinet.

Safety. Sudden urgency with pain, blood or fever needs assessment; any irregular bleeding deserves care — never use apiol-containing preparations.
