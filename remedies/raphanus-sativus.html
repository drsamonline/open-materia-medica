Raphanus

Made from the black radish — the winter root of the European kitchen, a genuine carminative and cholagogue of old European medicine, and the classical remedy of the trapped wind.

The whole picture, in one breath

Think of wind trapped after the surgeon's knife: retained gas after operations, the books' great post-operative colic, the distension and the urging that brings nothing, the dark root of the winter kitchen. The trapped-wind remedy: that is Raphanus.

The substance, in plain words

The black radish is the winter root of the European kitchen — eaten raw with salt for the liver and the digestion across France and Germany, a genuine carminative and cholagogue of old European medicine, its sulfur chemistry really moving the wind. The proving gave the root the trapped-gas portrait.

The traditional picture Classical sources

The inner state

  • Anxiety with the distension. The worry and discomfort of the trapped wind, worse at night.
  • Irritability. Cross from the constant pressure and the unsuccessful urging.
  • Restlessness. Unable to settle with the distended, gassy belly.

The body

  • Retained gas after operations. The keynote: wind retained after operations — the books' great post-operative colic, where the gut will not wake.
  • Distension and urging without result. The swollen, drum-tight abdomen with the urging that brings nothing.
  • Post-operative colic. The books' specific use — the colic of the recovering surgical belly.
  • Worse cold damp and eating. Wet weather and food restart the trapping.
  • Better for the wind passing and warmth. The wind's passage and warmth ease the colic.

Where it acts (organ affinities)

Bowels (gas, post-operative colic), gall and liver region, general vitality.

Where the classical picture comes from

The winter radish's proving gave the trapped-wind portrait; Raphanus became the classical remedy of the retained gas — the kitchen root that moved the wind serving the post-operative gut that could not.

The substance in real medicine Pharmacology

What this section is: the pharmacology and toxicology of the source substance. It is not clinical evidence that a homeopathic preparation of it treats any condition. What controlled trials do and do not show is set out on the Evidence page.

The black radish is genuinely one of old European medicine's real digestive remedies: eaten for the liver and the wind across the continent, its sulfur chemistry really does move the gut — a folk practice with a real mechanism. The post-operative ileus it portrayed is now modern surgery's own protocol: early waking, walking and real prokinetics.
